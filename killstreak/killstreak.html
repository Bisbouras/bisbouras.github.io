Killstreak

Godot 4, GDScript · 2026 · in progress

A top-down 1v1 shooter, built in Godot, where the map is always visible but the opponent is not: a vision cone decides what either player can actually see.

The pitch

Killstreak is a 1v1 shooter played from directly above. The camera shows the whole arena's geometry at all times, but the opponent, every gun on the ground, and every thrown weapon only render when they fall inside your vision cone, a slice of the map in front of wherever you are currently aiming. Checking a doorway means turning your gun away from whatever is in front of you right now.

It's a game of information: knowing where the opponent is, and whether they know where you are. No carryover between rounds, no progression, just a clean slate every time, which keeps the pacing arcade instead of building toward anything. Standing still is the most accurate way to shoot, but the map is built so that never moving loses anyway. Kills take real time too, the pistol runs about 1.5 to 2.5 seconds depending on range and the rifle roughly half that, long enough for whoever gets caught out to actually fight back.

Vision

The camera holds one fixed shot of the whole arena. Each player's view still rotates independently though, so their own spawn sits at the bottom and they always play upward, the way a fighting game keeps both players facing in. That flip only touches rendering and input, since the simulation runs on one shared coordinate frame that both peers have to agree on.

The vision cone is what actually hides things. The opponent, a gun on the ground, a thrown weapon: all of it exists in the world, but none of it draws to your screen unless it falls inside your cone, and the edge is a hard cutoff rather than a fade, so what you see is exactly what your position and aim earn you.

Sound covers the gap the cone leaves. Footsteps outside your vision draw as an arc of possible bearings instead of an exact position, narrower for a loud sound like running and wider for a quiet one like walking, with the true direction sitting off centre within that arc on purpose so you can't just aim at its middle. Reload, pickup, and gunfire events show up as a marker at roughly the right spot, since what matters there is that it happened.

Ground weapons

Everyone starts with a pistol. Every stronger weapon is a pickup on the ground, and you can only hold one gun at a time, so taking a new one means throwing your current one away first, which can leave you weaponless for the rest of the round since nothing respawns mid-round. A thrown weapon does real damage on impact, bounces off walls and cover, and keeps whatever ammo it had loaded, so throwing an empty pistol at someone can literally arm them. The Milestone 1 arena gates its two rifles behind a wall that cycles solid and open every 8 seconds, so getting to one is a matter of timing as much as positioning.

Networking

Milestone 1 runs networked over LAN from the start rather than as a local build retrofitted later, server-authoritative, with clients sending input and the server deciding what happened. Every piece of gameplay state, positions, health, ammo, even the map's own random numbers, was built from the beginning to be readable and restorable as a single snapshot, on a fixed timestep, with nothing hidden away in the scene tree. That discipline is what let internet play get pulled forward and actually work: adding client-side prediction for movement later was a matter of plugging a rollback library into an already-deterministic simulation, not restructuring one that wasn't.

The one place prediction and server authority have to meet is a shot. Movement is predicted locally so it feels instant, but hit detection stays fully server-side and unpredicted, since a game built around sudden, close-range reveals is the last place you want a guessed outcome. Instead the server keeps a short buffered history of where the opponent actually was and checks a shot against their position rewound by the shooter's own round-trip time, so real internet latency doesn't turn a clean-looking shot into a miss against a target that had already moved by the time it arrived.

Where it stands

Milestone 1, a LAN-playable prototype, is fully built: server-authoritative movement, vision-cone rendering and occlusion, hitscan combat, the ground-weapon pickup and throw loop, and round reset. Internet play was pulled forward ahead of schedule and runs through a dedicated host with client-side movement prediction and lag-compensated hit detection. The visual side of the audio system, the arcs and symbols, is built too. The actual sound hasn't been wired in yet.

What's left is the thing Milestone 1 exists to answer, and the part I'm most curious about: a full playtest on two separate machines, to see whether contesting weapons under vision cones produces real decisions or just two players holding angles.

What's next

The playtest comes first. The spread, TTK, and timing numbers in the design doc are starting points, and tuning them against real two-player sessions is Milestone 1's actual deliverable. After that, wiring real sound into the audio system that is already built for it, then round structure and the match-level streak win condition, with grenades and penetrable cover under consideration for later.
