Killstreak

Godot 4, GDScript · 2026 · in progress

A top-down 1v1 shooter, built in Godot, where the map is always visible but the opponent is not: a vision cone decides what either player can actually see.

The pitch

Killstreak is a 1v1 shooter played from directly above. The camera shows the whole arena's geometry at all times, but the opponent, every gun on the ground, and every thrown weapon only render when they fall inside your vision cone, a slice of the map in front of wherever you are currently aiming. Checking a doorway means turning your gun away from whatever is in front of you right now.

Health and ammo do not decide rounds here, information does: knowing where the opponent is, and whether they know where you are. Every weapon past the starting pistol sits on the ground instead of in a loadout, so contesting it is most of what a round is about. There is no carryover between rounds and no progression, just a clean slate each time, which keeps the pacing arcade instead of building toward anything across a match. Standing still is the most accurate way to shoot, but the map is built so that never moving loses anyway.

Vision

The camera is static and shows the whole arena at once, no scrolling, no zoom. Each player's view is rotated independently so their own spawn sits at the bottom and they always play upward, the way a fighting game keeps both players facing in. The flip only touches rendering and input. The simulation itself runs on one shared coordinate frame, which matters once the game is networked and both peers have to agree on what actually happened.

The cone is built from Godot's 2D lighting system rather than a hand-rolled visibility check, a spotlight standing in for a sightline, with wall geometry acting as the light blocker. Hidden means not drawn at all: hard-edged occlusion, no soft shadows, no falloff, so a visible edge always means something real about the geometry and not a lighting artifact. Cone angle sits at 50 degrees, widened from 45 after early testing felt too tight.

Sound covers the gap the cone leaves. Footsteps outside your vision draw as an arc of possible bearings instead of an exact position, narrower for a loud sound like running, wider for a quiet one like walking. The true direction sits off centre within that arc on purpose, so a player can't just learn to aim at its middle. Reload, pickup, and gunfire events draw as a marker instead, since what matters there is what happened, not roughly where.

Ground weapons

Every player starts with a pistol and nothing else. Every stronger weapon is a pickup placed by the map. The catch is that you can only ever hold one gun: taking a new one off the ground means throwing your current one away first, and with no mid-round respawns, losing that exchange can leave you weaponless for the rest of it. That cost is what makes holding an angle with the pistol a real alternative to walking into a contested pickup, rather than the game always forcing the issue.

Throwing isn't just a way to free your hands. A thrown weapon travels fast enough to do real damage on impact, bounces off walls and cover instead of stopping dead, and keeps whatever ammo was loaded when it left your hand. Throwing an empty pistol at someone and arming them with it is a legitimate, if desperate, way to lose.

Map geometry is the other lever. The Milestone 1 arena gates each side's rifle behind a wall that cycles solid and open on an 8 second period, telegraphed for about a second before it flips, so contesting the better weapon is a question of timing as much as positioning. It's a general tool available to any map rather than a rule baked into the system: a later map might use several walls on different timers, or none at all.

Time to kill

Time to kill is slower than a game like CS2 on purpose, and it drops further at range through a damage falloff curve. The pistol kills in about 1.5 seconds up close and 2.5 seconds at range, the rifle in 0.75 and 1.25. The reasoning is specific to the vision-cone format: with a fast kill, whoever spots the other player first simply wins, and every round collapses into who guessed the right angle, a coinflip dressed up as a gunfight. A longer TTK gives the player who gets caught out a real window to turn, dash, or break line of sight, which is also why reloading takes at least 1.5 seconds and is loud enough to give away your position, and why getting hit always shows a directional indicator toward the shooter, from any angle. An ambush should start a fight, not end it before it began.

Networking

Milestone 1 runs networked over LAN from the start rather than as a local build retrofitted later, server-authoritative, with clients sending input and the server deciding what happened. Every piece of gameplay state, positions, health, ammo, even the map's own random numbers, was built from the beginning to be readable and restorable as a single snapshot, on a fixed timestep, with nothing hidden away in the scene tree. That discipline is what let internet play get pulled forward and actually work: adding client-side prediction for movement later was a matter of plugging a rollback library into an already-deterministic simulation, not restructuring one that wasn't.

The one place prediction and server authority have to meet is a shot. Movement is predicted locally so it feels instant, but hit detection stays fully server-side and unpredicted, since a game built around sudden, close-range reveals is the last place you want a guessed outcome. Instead the server keeps a short buffered history of where the opponent actually was and checks a shot against their position rewound by the shooter's own round-trip time, so real internet latency doesn't turn a clean-looking shot into a miss against a target that had already moved by the time it arrived.

Where it stands

Milestone 1, a LAN-playable prototype, is fully built: server-authoritative movement, vision-cone rendering and occlusion, hitscan combat, the ground-weapon pickup and throw loop, and round reset. Internet play was pulled forward ahead of schedule and runs through a dedicated host with client-side movement prediction and lag-compensated hit detection. The visual side of the audio system, the arcs and symbols, is built too. The actual sound hasn't been wired in yet.

What's left is the thing Milestone 1 exists to answer, and the part I'm most curious about: a full playtest on two separate machines, to see whether contesting weapons under vision cones produces real decisions or just two players holding angles.

What's next

The playtest comes first. The spread, TTK, and timing numbers in the design doc are starting points, and tuning them against real two-player sessions is Milestone 1's actual deliverable. After that, wiring real sound into the audio system that is already built for it, then round structure and the match-level streak win condition, with grenades and penetrable cover under consideration for later.
